Real-world motion

Motion planning is everywhere

Every useful robot action depends on a feasible motion.

You may let the clips run for a moment to show how widely motion planning is used, from established industrial tasks to newer robot applications.

You may ask the room: “What has to go right for these motions to look so effortless?” The whole robot moving within its limits and avoiding obstacles could be useful points to draw out.

A possible lead-in to the Jaco example: “Now let’s see what happens when the motion fails.”

You may show this Jaco example to illustrate how motion planning can fail in the real world, even during an everyday task.

You may ask students what they would check before running the motion. You could give them a moment to suggest answers before bringing in joint limits, collisions, singularities and continuity.

A possible takeaway: reaching the target is only part of the problem. The robot needs a feasible path all the way there.

Why this matters now

From industry to everyday life

Robots have moved from industrial settings into everyday life.

Assistive and collaborative robots now interact directly with people.

Motion planning with guarantees is essential for safe human–robot interaction.

You could bring the Jaco failure back into the discussion: what would that unexpected motion mean for someone working closely with the robot?

You may emphasise why predictable motion matters when people share the workspace or depend on a robot for assistance. This could help students see why guarantees matter.

You could follow with: “What can we establish about a motion before we let the robot execute it?”

The engineering standard we will develop

Global analysis and guarantees

Feasibility over
the entire motion.

With explicit assumptions.
  1. The allowed robot posturesWhere do joint limits, collisions and singularities restrict motion?
  2. The connections between posturesWhich postures can the robot move between continuously?
  3. The evidence for executionWhich constraints hold along the path, and what remains unverified?

A guarantee is relative to the model and constraints. Checking isolated samples alone does not certify the motion between them.

You may make “guarantee” concrete by asking: what have we checked, over which part of the motion, and under what assumptions?

A useful example is a path with valid start and end postures but a collision in between. You could use it to explain why we need to reason about the entire motion and how the allowed postures connect.

You may want to clarify that these guarantees depend on the model and constraints. Safe execution also relies on sensing, control and operating conditions. Part I builds the tools for analysing the path.

You may use the Delta robot as a familiar entry point to parallel mechanisms, perhaps mentioning its EPFL origins with Reymond Clavel.

You could focus on what changes when several chains support the same platform: their motions have to satisfy the constraints together. This is why the arrangement of links and joints matters so much.

To lead into the interaction example, you may ask how the mechanism’s design affects what it feels like to move the robot by hand.
