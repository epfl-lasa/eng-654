1 · Fix the redundancy coordinate

Original joint 3 is fixed; reduced joint 3 is original joint 4

Keep both index systems visible

\[q_3=\phi=\frac{\pi}{6},\qquad (\theta_1,\theta_2,\theta_3)=(q_1,q_2,q_4).\]

The positioning chain has the three active coordinates \(\theta_1,\theta_2,\theta_3\). The wrist has \(q_5,q_6,q_7\). There are six unknown angles after fixing \(\phi\).

Define “home” for this six-joint member

Set all six active coordinates to zero while keeping \(q_3=\phi\). The wrist is still at \((0,0,1.14)^\top\), while the tool’s home orientation becomes \(R_z(\phi)\).

Changing \(\phi\) changes the six-joint kinematic model. A generic pose of the unrestricted seven-joint robot has a continuous IK family; the eight branches below are the isolated solutions at this fixed \(\phi\).

Throughout the position derivation, \(c_i=\cos\theta_i\), \(s_i=\sin\theta_i\). In particular, \(c_3=\cos q_4\), not \(\cos q_3\).

3 · Identify the wrist

The last three axes intersect at one point

At the new home configuration

\[p_{w,0}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\H+L+U\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\1.14\end{bmatrix},\qquad H=0.34,\ L=U=0.4.\]

Joints 5, 6 and 7 rotate about axes through this wrist point. Their motion changes the tool orientation without moving the wrist intersection.

For a desired tool pose

\[p_w=p_d-\ell R_d e_z,\qquad \ell=0.081+0.045=0.126.\]

The offset is along the desired tool’s \(z\)-axis. The 0.081 m final-joint offset and the 0.045 m fixed tool offset must both be included.

The wrist intersection has no unique orientation until a frame is chosen. Position partitioning requires the point, not an invented fixed wrist-to-tool rotation.

4 · Separate the positioning robot

Use the same point in either representation

D-H position chain

\[\begin{bmatrix}p_w\\1\end{bmatrix}=A_1(\theta_1)A_2(\theta_2)A_3(\phi)A_4(\theta_3)\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\U\\1\end{bmatrix}.\]

The point \((0,0,U)^\top\) is expressed in standard D-H frame 4, immediately after original joint 4.

PoE position chain

\[\begin{bmatrix}p_w\\1\end{bmatrix}=e^{\widehat S_1\theta_1}e^{\widehat S_2\theta_2}e^{\widehat{\bar S}_4\theta_3}\begin{bmatrix}p_{w,0}\\1\end{bmatrix}.\]

The three omitted wrist exponentials each fix \(p_{w,0}\). They can therefore be removed when computing this point, even though they are required for the complete tool pose.

The representations produce the same wrist-position equations. Switching between D-H and PoE changes how the model is written, not which IK solutions exist.

5–6 · Correct method choice

Choose Paden–Kahan from the arm geometry

Correct tutorial choice: Paden–Kahan subproblems

The first two active axes intersect at the shoulder, so \(a_1=0\). Both rotations preserve the shoulder-to-wrist distance. Paden–Kahan subproblem 3 (PK3) uses that distance to determine the elbow angle; Cramer’s rule then recovers the shoulder and base angles.

\[R=L^2+U^2+2LU\cos\theta_3.\]

Which algebraic step fails?

In the offset-arm derivation, one may recover a signed radial quantity through \(E=(R-F_3)/(2a_1)\). That division requires \(a_1\ne0\), and therefore cannot be used here.

This does not rule out all algebraic methods. The Paden–Kahan distance constraint also produces an equation, and Cramer’s rule is a valid back-substitution. The geometry makes a quartic elimination unnecessary.

Review the condition in Lecture 02, slide 30.

7 · Expand the wrist position

Separate the base rotation from the shoulder and elbow

Let \((x,y,z_w)=p_w\). Shift the shoulder height once: \(z=z_w-H\). Define \(\rho^2=x^2+y^2\) and \(R=\rho^2+z^2\); this scalar \(R\) is a squared distance, whereas \(R_d\) is a rotation matrix.

\[A=L+Uc_3,\qquad B=-Uc_\phi s_3,\qquad C=-Us_\phi s_3.\]
\[E=As_2+Bc_2,\qquad z=Ac_2-Bs_2.\]
\[\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}c_1&-s_1\\s_1&c_1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}E\\C\end{bmatrix}.\]

Thus \(x=c_1E-s_1C\) and \(y=s_1E+c_1C\). Rotation about axis 1 preserves the horizontal length: \(x^2+y^2=E^2+C^2\). The signed value \(E\) is not automatically the nonnegative radius \(\rho\).

8 · Recover reduced θ₃

PK3 removes two angles with a distance constraint

Translate the origin to the home elbow \(e=(0,0,H+L)^\top\). Set \(p=Ue_z\), \(q=-Le_z\), and \(\omega=(\sin\phi,-\cos\phi,0)^\top\). The PK3 constraint is \(\|\operatorname{Rot}(\omega,\theta_3)p-q\|=\sqrt R\). Here \(p\) is the home wrist relative to the elbow and \(q\) is the shoulder relative to the elbow; \(q\) denotes a point in this constraint, not a joint angle.

\[R=E^2+C^2+z^2=A^2+B^2+C^2=L^2+U^2+2LUc_3.\]

Both elbow signs

\[c_3=\frac{R-L^2-U^2}{2LU},\qquad s_3=\sigma_3\sqrt{1-c_3^2},\qquad\theta_3=\operatorname{atan2}(s_3,c_3),\quad\sigma_3\in\{-1,+1\}.\]

Keep the real-root test

For \(|c_3|<1\), there are two distinct elbow angles. At \(c_3=\pm1\), they meet. If \(|c_3|>1\) beyond numerical tolerance, the target wrist point is unreachable.

Now calculate \(A,B,C\) separately for each elbow sign. In particular, changing \(s_3\) changes the signs of both \(B\) and \(C\).

8 · Why two E values?

The measured horizontal distance does not determine a sign

\[\rho^2=E^2+C^2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad E=\sigma_E\sqrt{\rho^2-C^2},\qquad\sigma_E\in\{-1,+1\}.\]

For a chosen elbow sign, \(C\) is known. Intersect the circle of radius \(\rho\) with the line whose second coordinate equals \(C\). Two intersections give two signed first coordinates, \(E_-\) and \(E_+\).

Each pair \((E,C)\) is rotated by its own \(\theta_1\) onto the same desired \((x,y)\). The corresponding \(\theta_2\) is then recovered from \((E,z)\).

The negative sign is a distinct shoulder solution, not a negative physical length. If \(\rho^2=C^2\), the two values meet at \(E=0\). If \(\rho^2<C^2\), that elbow candidate gives no real shoulder solution.

8 · Back-substitute θ₂

Cramer’s rule gives cosine and sine together

\[\begin{bmatrix}B&A\\A&-B\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}c_2\\s_2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}E\\z\end{bmatrix},\qquad\det=-(A^2+B^2).\]
\[c_2=\frac{BE+Az}{A^2+B^2},\qquad s_2=\frac{AE-Bz}{A^2+B^2},\qquad\theta_2=\operatorname{atan2}(s_2,c_2).\]

Why the signs are fixed

The numerator for \(c_2\) is \(-BE-Az\); the numerator for \(s_2\) is \(Bz-AE\). Dividing each by the negative determinant gives the expressions at left.

Use the same \(E\), \(A\) and \(B\) branch throughout. Verify \(c_2^2+s_2^2=1\) and substitute into both original equations. There is no additional ± sign once this pair is known.

8 · Back-substitute θ₁

Apply the same two-equation idea to x and y

\[\begin{bmatrix}E&-C\\C&E\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}c_1\\s_1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix},\qquad\det=E^2+C^2=\rho^2.\]
\[c_1=\frac{Ex+Cy}{E^2+C^2},\qquad s_1=\frac{Ey-Cx}{E^2+C^2},\qquad\theta_1=\operatorname{atan2}(s_1,c_1).\]

Four generic arm branches

Enumerate \((\sigma_3,\sigma_E)\) with each sign positive or negative, keeping only real solutions. Rebuild \(p_w\) for every candidate.

Since \(C\) is generally nonzero for \(\phi=\pi/6\), \(\theta_1\) is generally not just \(\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)\). The equivalent geometric expression is:

\[\theta_1=\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)-\operatorname{atan2}(C,E).\]

9 · Wrist convention and relative orientation

Express the desired tool orientation in the solved arm frame

The correct multiplication order

\[R_d=R_{\mathrm{arm}}R_{\mathrm{wrist}},\qquad R_{\mathrm{wrist}}=R_{\mathrm{arm}}^\top R_d.\]

Here \(R_{\mathrm{arm}}={}^0R_{4,\mathrm{DH}}\), the orientation after original joint 4. It is also the third active arm frame if that reduced frame is chosen to coincide with this D-H frame.

Correct convention: intrinsic Z–Y–Z

\[R_{\mathrm{wrist}}=R_z(q_5)R_x(-\pi/2)R_z(q_6)R_x(\pi/2)R_z(q_7)=R_z(q_5)R_y(q_6)R_z(q_7).\]

The fixed tool transform is a translation, so it introduces no additional orientation factor. Compute a new residual matrix for each of the four arm solutions.

A PoE product of the three active exponentials alone is a displacement from home, not automatically the chosen arm frame. Include its home orientation before using the residual-rotation formula.

9 · Complete the rotation and solve the wrist

The bottom-right entry is cos q₆

On this slide only, write \(C_i=\cos q_i\), \(S_i=\sin q_i\) for original wrist joints \(i=5,6,7\), and denote the entries of \(R_{\mathrm{wrist}}\) by \(r_{ij}\).

\[R_{\mathrm{wrist}}=\begin{bmatrix}C_5C_6C_7-S_5S_7&-C_5C_6S_7-S_5C_7&C_5S_6\\S_5C_6C_7+C_5S_7&-S_5C_6S_7+C_5C_7&S_5S_6\\-S_6C_7&S_6S_7&\boxed{C_6}\end{bmatrix}.\]
\[s_6=\sigma_w\sqrt{r_{13}^2+r_{23}^2},\quad q_6=\operatorname{atan2}(s_6,r_{33}),\quad \sigma_w=\pm1.\]
\[q_5=\operatorname{atan2}(r_{23}/s_6,r_{13}/s_6),\quad q_7=\operatorname{atan2}(r_{32}/s_6,-r_{31}/s_6).\]

Two wrist branches per regular arm solution

The branches are related by \((q_5,q_6,q_7)\mapsto(q_5+\pi,-q_6,q_7+\pi)\), with angle wrapping for comparison.

The division by \(s_6\) retains the correct quadrant for both signs. At \(s_6=0\), these formulas must be replaced by a coupled-angle relation.

10 · One complete numerical back-substitution

Choose the negative elbow and positive E

Take \(\sigma_3=-1\), \(\sigma_E=+1\). The distance equation gives \(\theta_3=-1.1\) rad. Evaluate the coefficients for this elbow before solving the two linear systems.

\[A=0.581438449,\quad B=0.308723286,\quad C=0.178241472,\quad E=0.567104623,\quad z=0.334324817.\]

Recover the shoulder

\[c_2=\frac{BE+Az}{A^2+B^2}=0.852524522,\quad s_2=\frac{AE-Bz}{A^2+B^2}=0.522687229.\]
\[\theta_2=\operatorname{atan2}(0.522687229,0.852524522)=0.55\ \mathrm{rad}.\]

Recover the base rotation

\[c_1=\frac{Ex+Cy}{E^2+C^2}=0.939372713,\quad s_1=\frac{Ey-Cx}{E^2+C^2}=0.342897807.\]

The arm coordinates are \((q_1,q_2,q_4)=(0.35,0.55,-1.1)\). Its two wrist solutions are \((q_5,q_6,q_7)=(0.7,0.8,-0.45)\) and \((0.7-\pi,-0.8,\pi-0.45)\); both reproduce the complete target.

11 · Apply the actual joint limits

Pose closure and physical admissibility are different checks

Limits in the supplied robot

Original joints 1, 3 and 5: ±170°. Joints 2, 4 and 6: ±120°. Joint 7: ±175°. Use the URDF’s numerical radian bounds in code.

Test all seven coordinates, including the fixed \(q_3\). A mathematical IK branch that reproduces the pose may still exceed one or more joint limits.

The narrower-than-360° limits make a wrapped angle outside the listed interval infeasible; adding a full turn cannot move it inside that interval.

Reference classification

6 of 8 branches respect every joint limit.

Branches 2 and 8 are rejected: q7 = -178.902412°, below its −175° lower bound.

All other listed branches are admissible for the supplied joint limits.

For the student’s own FK target, recompute the classification from the new solutions. Do not carry the reference target’s yes/no answers to a different pose.

Validation and exceptional cases

State the conditions behind every division and branch count

Regular-case checks

Require \(|c_3|<1\), \(\rho^2-C^2>0\), \(A^2+B^2>0\), \(\rho^2>0\) and \(|s_6|>0\) for four distinct arm roots and two wrist roots each.

After solving, verify both \(\|p(q)-p_d\|_2\) and \(\|R(q)-R_d\|_F\), using documented tolerances. Compare joint vectors modulo \(2\pi\) to remove duplicate roots.

Do not divide through singularities

If \(A=B=0\), the shoulder equations may leave \(\theta_2\) free. If \(\rho=0\), the base angle can be undetermined. Report these as families or special cases, not as eight isolated solutions.

At \(q_6=0\), only \(q_5+q_7\) is fixed. At \(q_6=\pi\), only \(q_5-q_7\) is fixed. Choose a member of the wrist family only after considering the joint limits.
