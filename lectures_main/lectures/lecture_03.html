One target can have several—or no—IK solutions

Forward kinematics maps each joint vector to one position; inverse kinematics asks for the entire set of joint vectors that reach a target.

Empty

Algebraic method · Pieper’s elimination

Two invariants remove the first two joint angles

For the custom robot, \(\alpha_1=-\pi/2\), \(\alpha_2=\pi/2\). Write \(c_i=\cos\theta_i\), \(s_i=\sin\theta_i\).

First remove the base offset: \(z:=z_d-d_1\). Then \(R:=x^2+y^2+z^2\) and \(z\) are unchanged by rotation about joint 1, so \(\theta_1\) disappears.

Write the invariants as linear polynomials in \(c_2,s_2\)

\[\begin{aligned} R&=2a_1(F_1c_2+F_2s_2)+F_3,\\ z&=F_2c_2-F_1s_2. \end{aligned}\]
\[\begin{aligned} F_1&=a_2+a_3c_3,\quad F_2=d_3,\quad C=d_2+a_3s_3,\\ F_3&=a_1^2+F_1^2+F_2^2+C^2\\ &=a_1^2+a_2^2+a_3^2+d_2^2+d_3^2\\ &\quad+2a_2a_3c_3+2d_2a_3s_3. \end{aligned}\]

Each \(F_i\) is constant or affine in \(c_3,s_3\).

Eliminate \(c_2,s_2\), then solve for \(\theta_3\)

\[E:=\frac{R-F_3}{2a_1}=F_1c_2+F_2s_2\]

Square and add the equations for \(E\) and \(z\): the cross terms cancel and \(c_2^2+s_2^2=1\).

\[E^2+z^2=F_1^2+F_2^2.\]
\[t_3=\tan\frac{\theta_3}{2},\qquad c_3=\frac{1-t_3^2}{1+t_3^2},\quad s_3=\frac{2t_3}{1+t_3^2}\]

Substitute and clear \((1+t_3^2)^2\): a polynomial of degree at most four determines \(\theta_3\).

Here \(z_d\) is world height, \(z\) is shifted height, and \(R\) is a squared distance. Solve the quartic, then recover the other two angles by linear algebra.

Algebraic method · Back-substitution

Once \(\theta_3\) is known, recover \(\theta_2\) and \(\theta_1\)

Evaluate \(F_1,F_2,F_3,C\) at each real third-joint candidate. Cramer’s rule solves two \(2\times2\) systems.

1 · Obtain \(c_2,s_2\) from \(R,z\)

\[\begin{bmatrix}2a_1F_1&2a_1F_2\\F_2&-F_1\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}c_2\\s_2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}R-F_3\\z\end{bmatrix}\]
\[E=\frac{R-F_3}{2a_1},\qquad \Delta_2=F_1^2+F_2^2\]
\[\begin{aligned} c_2&=\frac{F_1E+F_2z}{\Delta_2},\\ s_2&=\frac{F_2E-F_1z}{\Delta_2},\\ \theta_2&=\operatorname{atan2}(s_2,c_2). \end{aligned}\]

2 · Obtain \(c_1,s_1\) from \(x,y\)

\[A=a_1+F_1c_2+F_2s_2,\qquad C=d_2+a_3s_3\]
\[\begin{bmatrix}A&-C\\C&A\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}c_1\\s_1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix},\qquad \Delta_1=A^2+C^2\]
\[\begin{aligned} c_1&=\frac{Ax+Cy}{\Delta_1},\\ s_1&=\frac{Ay-Cx}{\Delta_1},\\ \theta_1&=\operatorname{atan2}(s_1,c_1). \end{aligned}\]
Use these formulas when \(a_1\ne0\), \(\Delta_2\ne0\), and \(\Delta_1\ne0\). For every branch, enforce joint limits and verify the original world target \([x,y,z_d]^T\) by forward kinematics.

Custom 3R · 6 / 13 · Horizontal radius does not depend on joint 1

In \(x^2+y^2\), the first-joint terms cancel

Collect the parts of the coordinate equations that depend only on \(\theta_2,\theta_3\).

Custom 3R · 7 / 13 · See the first-joint invariants

Joint 1 moves the end effector along a circle around its axis

Hold \(\theta_2,\theta_3\) fixed and vary only \(\theta_1\). The circle through \(\mathbf p_d\) has constant height and constant radius.

Project onto the plane at height \(d_1\)

\[O_1=(0,0,d_1),\qquad \mathbf p_\perp=(x,y,d_1).\]
\[\begin{aligned} \|\mathbf p_\perp-O_1\|&=\rho=\sqrt{x^2+y^2},\\ z_d-d_1&=z. \end{aligned}\]

Changing \(\theta_1\) moves the point around these circles. It changes neither \(\rho\) nor \(z\).

Custom 3R · 10 / 13 · Eliminate the second joint

Eliminate \(s_2\), then use \(c_2^2+s_2^2=1\)

Set \(E=(R-F_3)/(2a_1)\). The equations are \(E=F_1c_2+F_2s_2\) and \(z=F_2c_2-F_1s_2\).

Custom 3R · 12 / 13 · Properties of the quartic

What do the roots of the quartic tell us?

If \(b_4\ne0\), \(P(t_3)\) has four complex roots counting multiplicity. Its real coefficients make nonreal roots occur in conjugate pairs.

Real roots give angle candidates

A generic quartic has 0, 2, or 4 real roots. Each real \(t_{3,j}\) gives \(\theta_{3,j}=2\operatorname{atan}(t_{3,j})\), modulo \(2\pi\).

Repeated roots can merge branches

A repeated root counts more than once algebraically. The number of distinct real candidates can therefore be 1 or 3 as well.

Check the missing half-angle point

\(\theta_3=\pi\) is not represented by finite \(t_3\). Test \((c_3,s_3)=(-1,0)\) in the closure; here this condition is \(b_4=0\). Then the finite polynomial has lower degree.

Solve, recover, then validate

Quartics admit formulas in radicals. In computation, use a polynomial root solver, recover \(\theta_2,\theta_1\), enforce joint limits, and check forward kinematics.

A real root is a third-joint candidate. The complete IK answer is the set of joint triples that survive back-substitution and validation.

Algebraic method · Cramer’s rule

Recover the second joint from the same two equations

At a real \(\theta_3\), evaluate the coefficients and solve for the two trigonometric unknowns.

\[\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}F_1&F_2\\F_2&-F_1\end{bmatrix}}_{K} \begin{bmatrix}c_2\\s_2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}E\\z\end{bmatrix},\qquad \det K=-(F_1^2+F_2^2).\]
\[\begin{aligned} c_2&=\frac{\det\begin{bmatrix}E&F_2\\z&-F_1\end{bmatrix}}{\det K} =\frac{F_1E+F_2z}{F_1^2+F_2^2},\\ s_2&=\frac{\det\begin{bmatrix}F_1&E\\F_2&z\end{bmatrix}}{\det K} =\frac{F_2E-F_1z}{F_1^2+F_2^2},\\ \theta_2&=\operatorname{atan2}(s_2,c_2). \end{aligned}\]
Closure

\(E^2+z^2=F_1^2+F_2^2\) guarantees a unit \((c_2,s_2)\).

Invertibility

Here \(F_2=0.75\), so \(F_1^2+F_2^2>0\) for every \(\theta_3\).

Algebraic method · Interpret the recovered \(\theta_2\)

Cramer’s solution also has a planar-angle interpretation

Using physical shifted height \(z\), the vector \((F_1,F_2)\) rotates into \((E,z)\) through \(-\theta_2\) in the displayed \(x\)–\(z\) plane.

\[\begin{bmatrix}E\\z\end{bmatrix}= \begin{bmatrix}c_2&s_2\\-s_2&c_2\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}F_1\\F_2\end{bmatrix}\]

The two vectors have the same length by the quartic closure.

\[\boxed{\theta_2=\operatorname{atan2}(F_2,F_1)-\operatorname{atan2}(z,E)}\]

This is the same angle obtained from \(\operatorname{atan2}(s_2,c_2)\) after Cramer’s rule.

The minus sign follows from \(\alpha_1=-90^\circ\): positive rotation about joint 2 is clockwise in this \(x\)–\(z\) coordinate plot.

Algebraic method · Recover \(\theta_1\)

Substitute \(\theta_2,\theta_3\), then solve the \(x,y\) equations

Set \(A=a_1+F_1c_2+F_2s_2=a_1+E\) and \(C=d_2+a_3s_3\). Only \(c_1,s_1\) remain unknown.

\[\begin{bmatrix}A&-C\\C&A\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}c_1\\s_1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}\]

\[c_1=\frac{Ax+Cy}{A^2+C^2},\qquad s_1=\frac{Ay-Cx}{A^2+C^2}.\]

\[\theta_1=\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)-\operatorname{atan2}(C,A).\]

If \(A^2+C^2=0\), the target is on axis 1 and these equations cannot determine \(\theta_1\).

Algebraic method · Feasibility and special structure

Feasibility tests and the intersecting-axis limit

The coefficient formulation identifies singular recovery cases explicitly; intersecting first and second axes change how we calculate \(E\).

\(a_1=0\)

The common-normal distance between axes 1 and 2 vanishes. For the nonparallel axes in this model, that means the axes intersect; coincident axes require a separate case.

\[E=\frac{R-F_3}{2a_1}\quad\text{is undefined.}\]

Interpretation: the distance equation becomes \(R=F_3\), independent of \(\theta_2\). Recover \(E=\pm\sqrt{F_1^2+F_2^2-z^2}\), then reuse Cramer’s rule.

\(F_1^2+F_2^2=0\)

The coefficient matrix for recovering \(c_2,s_2\) loses rank.

\[\det\!\begin{bmatrix}F_1&F_2\\F_2&-F_1\end{bmatrix}=-(F_1^2+F_2^2).\]

Conclusion: \(\theta_2\) is not identifiable from these two invariant equations.

The analytical procedure reduces position IK to one quartic solve, two angle recoveries, and forward-kinematics validation.

Geometric method · Circle primitive

Circular orbits of revolute motion

Keep \(\mathbf p\) fixed and decompose it into components along and perpendicular to the unit axis \(\boldsymbol\omega\). The sheet shows its orbit plane.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf p_\parallel&=(\boldsymbol\omega^T\mathbf p)\boldsymbol\omega,\\ \mathbf p_\perp&=\mathbf p-\mathbf p_\parallel,\\ \mathbf p&=\mathbf p_\parallel+\mathbf p_\perp. \end{aligned} \]
center\(\mathbf p_\parallel\)
radius\(r=\|\mathbf p_\perp\|\)
plane\(\boldsymbol\omega^T\mathbf x=\boldsymbol\omega^T\mathbf p\)
Rotation preserves axial coordinate, distance to the axis, and Euclidean length.

Geometric method · Circle–circle distance

Circle intersection and geometric feasibility

For coplanar circles with radii \(r_1,r_2\) and center distance \(d\), feasibility is determined by three lengths.

\(d>r_1+r_2\) or \(d<|r_1-r_2|\)

no intersection

\(d=r_1+r_2\) or \(d=|r_1-r_2|>0\)

one tangent point

\(|r_1-r_2|<d<r_1+r_2\)

two intersections

\(d=0,\ r_1=r_2>0\)

coincident circles: infinitely many points

Geometric method · PK1 statement

Paden–Kahan subproblem 1: point registration

Given unit axis \(\boldsymbol\omega\) and points \(\mathbf p,\mathbf q\), find \(\theta\) such that \(R(\boldsymbol\omega,\theta)\mathbf p=\mathbf q\).

PK1
\[R(\boldsymbol\omega,\theta)\mathbf p=\mathbf q\]

Exact feasibility requires

\[ \boldsymbol\omega^T\mathbf p=\boldsymbol\omega^T\mathbf q, \qquad \|\mathbf p_\perp\|=\|\mathbf q_\perp\|. \]
If feasible and the orbit radius is nonzero, PK1 has one solution modulo \(2\pi\).

Geometric method · PK1 solution

Cosine measures alignment; sine measures signed rotation

For a feasible PK1 problem, the projected vectors have the same nonzero length \(r\). The unit axis \(\boldsymbol\omega\) sets the positive rotation direction.

Build coordinates in the orbit plane

\[\begin{aligned} \mathbf p_\perp&=(I-\boldsymbol\omega\boldsymbol\omega^T)\mathbf p,\\ \mathbf q_\perp&=(I-\boldsymbol\omega\boldsymbol\omega^T)\mathbf q,\\ \mathbf e_1&=\mathbf p_\perp/r,\qquad \mathbf e_2=\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf e_1. \end{aligned}\]

\(\mathbf e_1\) points toward the starting point. \(\mathbf e_2\) is the positive tangent direction given by the right-hand rule.

\[\mathbf q_\perp=r\cos\theta\,\mathbf e_1+r\sin\theta\,\mathbf e_2.\]

Take dot products with these two perpendicular unit vectors to read the coordinates of \(\mathbf q_\perp\).

Extract cosine and signed sine

\[\begin{aligned} \cos\theta&=\frac{\mathbf e_1^T\mathbf q_\perp}{r} =\frac{\mathbf p_\perp^T\mathbf q_\perp}{r^2},\\ \sin\theta&=\frac{\mathbf e_2^T\mathbf q_\perp}{r} =\frac{(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf p_\perp)^T\mathbf q_\perp}{r^2}\\ &=\frac{\boldsymbol\omega^T(\mathbf p_\perp\times\mathbf q_\perp)}{r^2}. \end{aligned}\]

The dot product gives alignment. The scalar triple product gives signed area: positive when rotation from \(\mathbf p_\perp\) to \(\mathbf q_\perp\) follows \(+\boldsymbol\omega\).

\[\theta=\operatorname{atan2}\!\left( \boldsymbol\omega^T(\mathbf p_\perp\times\mathbf q_\perp),\, \mathbf p_\perp^T\mathbf q_\perp\right).\]

The common positive factor \(r^2\) cancels in \(\operatorname{atan2}\), which keeps the sign and quadrant.

Check: \(\boldsymbol\omega=\mathbf e_z\), \(\mathbf p_\perp=\mathbf e_x\), \(\mathbf q_\perp=\mathbf e_y\) gives \((\cos\theta,\sin\theta)=(0,1)\), so \(\theta=+90^\circ\). If \(r=0\), the point does not move: the angle is arbitrary or the target is infeasible.

Geometric method · PK3 statement

Paden–Kahan subproblem 3: a distance constraint

By how much should we rotate \(\mathbf p\) about \(\boldsymbol\omega\) so that it ends up exactly \(\delta\) away from \(\mathbf q\)?

PK3
\[\|R(\boldsymbol\omega,\theta)\mathbf p-\mathbf q\|=\delta\]

The norm is the straight-line distance from the rotated point \(R(\boldsymbol\omega,\theta)\mathbf p\) to \(\mathbf q\).

1

Place \(\mathbf p,\mathbf q\), then draw a radius \(\delta\) from \(\mathbf q\).

2

Sweep that radius to form the sphere of points at distance \(\delta\) from \(\mathbf q\).

3

Rotate \(\mathbf p\) to draw its orbit; slice the sphere by this plane and mark the circle intersections.

Geometric method · PK3 reduction

Planar reduction of the PK3 constraint

Axial separation consumes part of the allowed distance; the remainder becomes an in-plane circle radius.

\[\begin{aligned} \mathbf c&=(\boldsymbol\omega^T\mathbf p)\boldsymbol\omega,\quad r=\|\mathbf p-\mathbf c\|,\\ h&=\boldsymbol\omega^T(\mathbf q-\mathbf c),\\ \mathbf q_\pi&=\mathbf q-h\boldsymbol\omega,\\ \mathbf q_\perp&=\mathbf q_\pi-\mathbf c,\quad d=\|\mathbf q_\perp\|. \end{aligned}\]

For a point \(\mathbf x\) in the orbit plane, the vertical and planar distances form a right triangle:

\[\begin{aligned} \|\mathbf x-\mathbf q\|^2&=h^2+\|\mathbf x-\mathbf q_\pi\|^2,\\ \delta_\perp&=\sqrt{\delta^2-h^2}. \end{aligned}\]

Check \(\delta^2\ge h^2\), then \(|r-\delta_\perp|\le d\le r+\delta_\perp\).

Shown: \(h=0.8\), \(\delta\simeq1.7443\), and \(\delta_\perp=1.55\).

Slice the sphere at the orbit plane. The section has center \(\mathbf q_\pi\) and radius \(\delta_\perp\); its intersections with the orbit are exactly the original circle–sphere intersections.

Geometric method · PK3 solution

Two PK3 branches from the law of cosines

Let \(\gamma\) rotate \(\mathbf p_\perp\) toward the second circle’s center. Let \(\phi\) be the triangle’s remaining angle.

\[ \gamma=\operatorname{atan2}\!\left( \boldsymbol\omega^T(\mathbf p_\perp\times\mathbf q_\perp), \mathbf p_\perp^T\mathbf q_\perp\right) \]
\[ \phi=\arccos\!\left(\frac{r^2+d^2-\delta_\perp^2}{2rd}\right) \]
\[\boxed{\theta=\gamma\pm\phi}\]
two\(0<\phi<\pi\)
one\(\phi=0\) or \(\pi\): tangent
noneacos argument outside \([-1,1]\)
Clamp within floating-point tolerance before \(\arccos\); reject larger feasibility violations. Handle \(r=0\) or \(d=0\) separately because the formula divides by \(2rd\).

Synthesis

Algebraic and geometric IK: a comparison

Both approaches eliminate rotations through invariants, but they expose solution structure in different mathematical representations.

D–H ordered elimination

  • generic \(a_1\neq0,\ s_{\alpha1}\neq0\);
  • invariants eliminate \(\theta_1,\theta_2\);
  • a quartic enumerates \(\theta_3\);
  • Cramer’s rule recovers \(\theta_2,\theta_1\).

Paden–Kahan decomposition

  • exploits \(a_1=0\), intersecting axes;
  • fixed points cancel joint motions;
  • circle intersections expose feasibility;
  • PK3 → both real signs of \(E\) → the same Cramer formulas.
Common principle: eliminate a rotation with something it cannot change—distance, axial projection, or a point on its axis.

Takeaways

Structural principles of analytical 3R inverse kinematics

Radial invariance, norm preservation, angle back-substitution, and fixed-point geometry provide the main analytical tools.

1

Shift \(z=z_d-d_1\), then use \(R=x^2+y^2+z^2\) to eliminate \(\theta_1\).

2

Write \(R\) and \(z\) as coefficient equations in \(c_2,s_2\); squaring and adding eliminates \(\theta_2\).

3

Cramer’s rule recovers each cosine and sine; \(\operatorname{atan2}\) gives the joint angle.

4

For this \(a_1=0\) robot, use PK3, calculate both real signs of \(E\), then reuse the same back-substitution: up to four branches.
