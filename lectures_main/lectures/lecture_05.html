Robot Singularities

From poses to motions: the Jacobian, its determinant, and the paths a robot can follow

Running examples: custom_3R_new.urdf for position kinematics and custom_6R_new.urdf for full-pose singularities.

From poses to motions

We know where the robot can be. How can it move?

Lectures 02–04 described poses and solved forward and inverse kinematics. Motion planning connects those poses through time.

So far: a configuration

\[\mathbf q\longmapsto\mathbf p=f(\mathbf q)\]

Which tool position belongs to these joint angles?

Which joint configurations reach a given pose?

Now: a motion

\[\mathbf q(t)\longmapsto\mathbf p(t)\]

Which joint rates move the tool along a desired path?

Can the robot maintain that motion at every point?

pose→nearby pose→continuous path→motion through time
The missing link is the relation between joint velocity and tool velocity.

Position → differential → velocity

Velocity is the time derivative of position

Position
\[\mathbf p(t)=f(\mathbf q(t))\]
Differential
\[d\mathbf p=\underbrace{\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf q}}_{J_p(\mathbf q)}\,d\mathbf q\]
Chain rule
\[\dot{\mathbf p}=\frac{d\mathbf p}{dt}=J_p(\mathbf q)\,\dot{\mathbf q}\]
The Jacobian is the local linear map: \(\Delta\mathbf p\approx J_p(\mathbf q)\Delta\mathbf q\) for a small joint displacement. The velocity relation is exact at the current configuration.

A quick example · Planar 2R

Differentiate the two position equations

Forward kinematics
\[\begin{aligned}x&=l_1\cos q_1+l_2\cos(q_1+q_2)\\y&=l_1\sin q_1+l_2\sin(q_1+q_2)\end{aligned}\]
Differentiate in time
\[\begin{aligned}\dot x&=-l_1s_1\dot q_1-l_2s_{12}(\dot q_1+\dot q_2)\\\dot y&=l_1c_1\dot q_1+l_2c_{12}(\dot q_1+\dot q_2)\end{aligned}\]
Collect the joint rates
\[\begin{bmatrix}\dot x\\\dot y\end{bmatrix}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}-l_1s_1-l_2s_{12}&-l_2s_{12}\\l_1c_1+l_2c_{12}&l_2c_{12}\end{bmatrix}}_{J_p(\mathbf q)}\begin{bmatrix}\dot q_1\\\dot q_2\end{bmatrix}\]

\(s_1=\sin q_1,\ c_1=\cos q_1,\ s_{12}=\sin(q_1+q_2),\ c_{12}=\cos(q_1+q_2)\).

The central object for robot motion

The Jacobian is at the heart of motion planning

It tells us which tool velocities are possible and which joint rates produce them: \(\dot{\mathbf p}=\mathbf J_1\dot q_1+\mathbf J_2\dot q_2\).

Try it: change q₂ toward 0°. Watch the two velocity columns become parallel and the velocity ellipse flatten. Can the tool still move in every planar direction?

One determinant value is decisive

At det J = 0, independent motion directions collapse

Expand the 2 × 2 determinant
\[\begin{aligned}\det J_p&=(-l_1s_1-l_2s_{12})l_2c_{12}+l_2s_{12}(l_1c_1+l_2c_{12})\\&=l_1l_2(s_{12}c_1-s_1c_{12})=l_1l_2\sin q_2.\end{aligned}\]

For \(l_1,l_2>0\)

\(q_2=0\) or \(\pi\pmod{2\pi}\) makes the links collinear.

The area spanned by the two columns is zero. The velocity ellipse becomes a line.

See the dependence at \(q_2=0\)

\[J_p=\begin{bmatrix}-\sin q_1\\\cos q_1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}l_1+l_2&l_2\end{bmatrix}\]

Both columns point along the same tangent: rank 1. Instantaneous radial tool motion is unavailable.

For a square task Jacobian, \(\det J=0\iff\operatorname{rank}J\) is below the task dimension. We now seek this zero set; determinant magnitude alone is not a universal measure of conditioning.

Recall the adjoint · Lecture 02

Choose coordinates that make the determinant easy

To locate singularities, we need the zeros of the determinant. Rigid changes of twist coordinates preserve them—and preserve the determinant itself.

Recall angular-first screw coordinates

\[\operatorname{Ad}_T=\begin{bmatrix}R&0\\{[\mathbf t]_\times R}&R\end{bmatrix}\]
\[\det\operatorname{Ad}_T=(\det R)^2=1\]

For a full 6 × 6 twist Jacobian

\[J'=\operatorname{Ad}_T J\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\det J'=\det J\]

The entries change; invertibility and rank do not.

Keep the same component order in both representations.

For a 3 × 3 position Jacobian at the same physical point, rotating components gives \(J'_p=RJ_p\) and the same determinant. Moving that task point changes the position task; this is a different operation.

Next we build columns from axes and lever arms, then use a convenient frame and reference point for the full twist.

The intuitive construction · Geometric Jacobian

Each column is a familiar screw acting at the tool

Recall the world-frame revolute screw from Lecture 02. At the current configuration, let \(\mathbf z_k\) be the unit axis and \(\mathbf p_k\) a point on it.

World-frame screw, angular first

\[\boldsymbol\xi_k^W=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf z_k\\\mathbf v_k^s\end{bmatrix},\qquad\mathbf v_k^s=-\mathbf z_k\times\mathbf p_k\]

\(\mathbf v_k^s\) describes the velocity field at the world origin; it is not generally the tool-point velocity.

Evaluate that motion at \(\mathbf p\)

\[\mathbf J_{p,k}=\mathbf v_k^s+\mathbf z_k\times\mathbf p=\mathbf z_k\times(\mathbf p-\mathbf p_k)\]

One joint rotating at unit rate produces a tangent velocity at the tool. That velocity is one Jacobian column.

\[\underbrace{J_k=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf z_k\times(\mathbf p-\mathbf p_k)\\\mathbf z_k\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{revolute, linear first}}\qquad\underbrace{J_k=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf z_k\\\mathbf0\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{prismatic, linear first}}\]
Use axes at the current configuration. Lecture 02’s stored home screws must first be propagated through the preceding joint motions. Now apply this construction to the course 3R robot.

Act IV · Apply the adjoint freedom

Move the twist reference to the wrist center

For the same rigid-body motion, let \(\mathbf r=\mathbf p_{\rm new}-\mathbf p_{\rm old}\). We now use linear-first components.

\[\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf v_{\rm new}\\\boldsymbol\omega\end{bmatrix}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}I&-[\mathbf r]_\times\\0&I\end{bmatrix}}_{P(\mathbf r)}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf v_{\rm old}\\\boldsymbol\omega\end{bmatrix}\]

Shift, then rotate the components

\[J'=\operatorname{diag}(R,R)P(\mathbf r)J\]

Both maps are invertible and have determinant 1.

Same rank and determinant

\[\det J'=\det J,\qquad\operatorname{rank}J'=\operatorname{rank}J\]

We may simplify the full 6 × 6 matrix before finding its zeros.

Choose the wrist center to create zeros, then a nearby frame to simplify the remaining entries. These freedoms change the representation of a full twist.

Act IV · Derive det A, step 1

Expand along the row with a zero

Abbreviate \(F=d_4c_3-a_3s_3\), \(H=a_3c_3+d_4s_3\), \(L=a_1+a_2c_2\), and \(K=a_2+H\). Here \(s_\alpha=\sin\alpha_2\), \(c_\alpha=\cos\alpha_2\).

\[A=\begin{bmatrix}-s_\alpha(Ls_3+d_4c_2)&c_\alpha(a_2s_3+d_4)&d_4\\-c_\alpha(L+c_2H)-s_2F&-s_\alpha K&0\\s_\alpha(Lc_3+a_3c_2)&-c_\alpha(a_2c_3+a_3)&-a_3\end{bmatrix}\]
\[\det A=-A_{21}M_{21}+A_{22}M_{22}\]

Delete row 2, column 1

\[\begin{aligned}M_{21}&=-a_3A_{12}-d_4A_{32}\\&=c_\alpha a_2(d_4c_3-a_3s_3)\\&=c_\alpha a_2F\end{aligned}\]

Delete row 2, column 2

\[\begin{aligned}M_{22}&=-a_3A_{11}-d_4A_{31}\\&=s_\alpha L(a_3s_3-d_4c_3)\\&=-s_\alpha LF\end{aligned}\]
Both minors contain F. The first arm singularity factor has emerged directly from the matrix.

Act IV · Derive det A, step 2

Collect the remaining terms into G

Continue with \(F=d_4c_3-a_3s_3\), \(H=a_3c_3+d_4s_3\), \(L=a_1+a_2c_2\), \(K=a_2+H\).

Substitute the two minors
\[\begin{aligned}\det A&=-[-c_\alpha(L+c_2H)-s_2F](c_\alpha a_2F)+(-s_\alpha K)(-s_\alpha LF)\\&=F\left[c_\alpha^2a_2(L+c_2H)+c_\alpha a_2s_2F+s_\alpha^2LK\right].\end{aligned}\]
Use L, K and \(c_\alpha^2+s_\alpha^2=1\)
\[\begin{aligned}G&=a_2L+H(c_\alpha^2a_2c_2+s_\alpha^2L)+c_\alpha a_2s_2F\\&=a_1a_2+a_1s_\alpha^2H+a_2c_2(a_2+H)+a_2s_2c_\alpha F.\end{aligned}\]
Assemble the full determinant
\[\det J=\underbrace{FG}_{\det A}\;\underbrace{s_5}_{\det W}\]
The arm contributes two factors; the wrist contributes one. The block B is absent because the full matrix is block triangular.

Act IV · Keep the definitions separate

Analytic orientation rates are not angular velocity

Analytic

For \(\mathbf x=[\mathbf p;\boldsymbol\phi]\):

\(\dot{\mathbf x}=J_A(\mathbf q)\dot{\mathbf q}\)

Geometric

For a twist:

\([\mathbf v;\boldsymbol\omega]=J_G(\mathbf q)\dot{\mathbf q}\)

\[J_A=T(\boldsymbol\phi)J_G\]
Rank equivalence requires the orientation-rate map \(T(\boldsymbol\phi)\) to be nonsingular. An Euler-angle chart singularity can make \(J_A\) singular even when the robot’s geometric Jacobian is regular. For the 3R Cartesian position task, the translational blocks coincide exactly.

From a position formula to a singularity equation

  1. Singularity is loss of instantaneous task mobility.
  2. Each Jacobian column is one joint-generated motion.

Motion-planning consequence

Near-singular paths amplify joint motion before rank is lost

\[\sigma_{\min}(J)>0:\qquad \|J^\dagger\|_2=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\min}(J)}\]

Conditioning matters

As \(\sigma_{\min}\to0\), some task-velocity directions demand rapidly growing joint rates.

At \(\sigma_{\min}=0\), the Jacobian loses rank and exact local inversion fails.

Path placement

A translated or rotated workspace path can move closer to—or farther from—a singular locus.

Branch continuation

Branches may meet, terminate, or require a coordinated switch at rank loss.

Planning metric

A regular Jacobian answers a local question

It lets us continue a motion nearby. Planning a whole path also requires the global arrangement of singularities and IK solutions.

1 · Find the singular set

\(\mathcal S=\{\mathbf q:\det J(\mathbf q)=0\}\). At this set, local task inversion fails.

2 · Follow regular configurations

A connected component of \(\mathcal C\setminus\mathcal S\) is an aspect. Check conditioning along the entire path, not just its endpoints.

3 · Understand the IK solutions

One aspect need not contain only one IK solution for a given pose. A local Jacobian alone cannot settle that global question.
